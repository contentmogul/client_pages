Perk Coffee · subscriber retention · two years around the 2023 price increase

What the last price increase cost, and what to assume next time

On 10 November 2023 the first price-increase notice went to subscribers. This report tracks retention across twelve months either side — twenty-four consecutive 30-day windows — and separates the price effect from two things that would otherwise be mistaken for it.

Singapore 2.4–4.5% of the established base lost, over and above the normal rate concentrated in the first 60 days, recovered by day 90
Malaysia 2.0–6.2% of the established base lost, over and above the normal rate same shape, wider range because the underlying trend is steeper

Two things that look like a price effect and are not

1. Retention was improving anyway, throughout both years

Across the twelve months before the notice, established-subscriber retention was rising steadily — about +0.20 points every 30 days in Singapore and +0.31 in Malaysia. A plain before-and-after comparison therefore flatters the price increase: on the raw twelve-month split, retention is higher after the increase than before it. That is the trend, not the price. Everything below is measured against where retention was already heading.

2. The free-trial restart pulled the averages down

Malaysia restarted trial acquisition in August 2023, taking 1,121 new subscribers in a single 30-day window against 40–80 in the months before. Trial joiners churn far harder than a settled subscriber. As that intake matured it entered the “established” group and dragged its average down — a change in who is in the group, not in how loyal anyone is.

The fix is a fixed cohort: measure only subscribers who joined before November 2022, so nobody acquired during the period can enter. The chart below shows both, and the distance between the lines is the size of that distortion.

Most of what you can see in the charts was delay, not departure

Two questions can be asked of the same orders. Did the next order arrive on schedule — within the subscriber’s own delivery cycle plus a fortnight? And did it ever arrive at all? The gap between the answers is people ordering late rather than leaving.

The second month is the same shape — 85% delay in both markets — and by the third month both measures are back above their pre-notice level. The disruption to ordering rhythm was large; the permanent loss behind it was small.

Every headline figure and the planning numbers below use the ever ordered again measure, so they describe genuine departures only.

Recommendation: plan on 3.5% in Singapore and 4% in Malaysia, with 7–8% carried as a downside case. Malaysia’s figure sits higher despite a similar measured effect because the increase proposed there is roughly twice as large in percentage terms. These land in the same place as an earlier six-month analysis that used a different method, which is reassuring — two routes, one answer.

Three reasons to treat these as a floor rather than a forecast

Today’s subscribers already survived one increase. The base now consists of people who did not leave in 2023, so it is likely less price-sensitive than the group that faced it.

A bigger rise may not scale in a straight line. The figures assume loss grows in proportion to the size of the increase; larger increases often cost more than proportionally, and there is only one past event to learn from.

The range is wide because the underlying trend is strong. Retention was moving quickly on its own, so how much of the post-notice shortfall you attribute to price depends on how far forward you project that trend. Both ends of the range are defensible.

How this was measured

  • Every figure comes from actual order history. A subscriber counts as retained if their next order actually arrived — not from a subscription status flag.
  • Twelve months either side, so the before-period covers the same calendar months as the after-period and seasonal movement largely cancels out.
  • “Established” means three or more previous orders, and the headline numbers use a fixed cohort that predates the whole period, so acquisition changes cannot move them.
  • The comparison point is the date subscribers were told, not the date the new price applied. Behaviour responds to the notice.
  • Every window has at least eighteen months of subsequent order history, so a subscriber counted as lost genuinely never returned.
  • Where a schedule is referred to, it is that subscriber’s own delivery frequency — these run from one to five weeks, so a single fixed window would compare subscribers on unequal terms and understate anyone on a longer plan.
